## Executive Summary

Labor matching models became a dominant macro labor framework because they provide a disciplined equilibrium account of unemployment and vacancies while remaining tractable for quantitative macro analysis (Pissarides 2000; Shimer 2005). The Diamond-Mortensen-Pissarides tradition supplied a common language for job creation, job destruction, wage setting, and search congestion, and this language has remained central even as model variants proliferated.

The field's most important unresolved tension is between local and global equilibrium perspectives. Many matching models are built on individual optimization and sometimes submarket-level equilibrium conditions that are coherent in isolation. But macroeconomic applications require consistency with aggregate general equilibrium conditions involving production, intertemporal allocation, policy, and often financial structure. That translation from local search equilibrium to economy-wide equilibrium is where many controversies over volatility, policy incidence, and welfare persist.

## Main Conclusions

1. Matching theory is foundational, not peripheral.
Search frictions are now widely treated as core to understanding unemployment dynamics and labor flows, not as a marginal adjustment around Walrasian labor markets.

2. DMP remains the benchmark architecture.
Even critiques of DMP often extend rather than replace it. The benchmark's portability across macro environments helps explain its continued influence.

3. Submarket approaches improved micro realism.
Directed-search models made clear that market segmentation, queueing by contract terms, and composition effects can matter for both efficiency and dynamics (Moen 1997; Acemoglu and Shimer 1999).

4. Aggregate fit requires complementary mechanisms.
The unemployment-vacancy volatility debate showed that canonical matching blocks often need additional wage-setting or propagation mechanisms to match observed cyclical patterns (Shimer 2005; Hall 2005; Hagedorn and Manovskii 2008).

5. Efficiency results are conditional.
Local efficiency benchmarks such as Hosios are analytically powerful but do not by themselves settle welfare or policy questions in richer macro GE environments.

## Representative Assessment of the Field

A representative summary of the literature is that there is broad consensus on the usefulness and necessity of matching frictions, paired with durable disagreement on model closure choices:
- How wages are determined in practice.
- How much heterogeneity and submarket structure is required.
- Which non-labor margins must be integrated to recover empirical dynamics.

Accordingly, the dominant framework today is best understood as a matching-centered family of models rather than one fixed specification.

## Implications for Future Work

The most productive frontier is not abandoning matching models, but tightening the bridge between submarket-level equilibrium detail and macro-level GE discipline. This includes:
1. Better aggregation from heterogeneous/submarket environments.
2. Joint modeling of wage institutions and vacancy creation.
3. Stronger integration with household heterogeneity and policy design.

These directions preserve the field's main achievement - an equilibrium theory of unemployment with explicit frictions - while addressing the central tension highlighted in this report.
