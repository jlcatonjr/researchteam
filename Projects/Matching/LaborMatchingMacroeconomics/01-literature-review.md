# Literature Review: Labor Matching Models in Macroeconomics

## 1. Origins: Search Frictions and Unemployment as an Equilibrium Outcome

The early search literature reframed unemployment from a pure disequilibrium residual into an equilibrium outcome under costly information and time-consuming matching. Stigler (1962) established job search as an information problem, and the broader search-theoretic tradition in labor economics developed around optimal stopping and reservation wages. This line made it possible for macroeconomists to treat unemployment as jointly determined by worker behavior, firm behavior, and market frictions rather than as a simple failure of wage adjustment.

A second early strand introduced dynamic labor-market adjustment with heterogeneity and mobility frictions. Lucas and Prescott (1974) provided a key macro bridge by showing how sectoral shocks can generate unemployment and reallocation dynamics even when agents optimize.

## 2. Foundational Matching Theory and the DMP Core

The central theoretical architecture emerged across Diamond (1971, 1982), Mortensen (1970, 1982), and Pissarides (1979, 1985), later synthesized in Pissarides (2000). The core ingredients are now standard:
- A matching function linking vacancies and unemployment to job-finding and vacancy-filling rates.
- Endogenous job creation from free entry and expected surplus.
- Wage determination, typically via Nash bargaining.
- Job destruction and idiosyncratic shocks.

Mortensen and Pissarides (1994) became the canonical stochastic version used in macro applications. It formalized joint flows in and out of employment under aggregate and idiosyncratic shocks, and it provided a tractable recursive equilibrium structure.

## 3. Efficiency and Externalities

A decisive theoretical benchmark is Hosios (1990): constrained efficiency in decentralized search equilibria requires that bargaining shares equal matching elasticities. This result clarified that equilibrium unemployment in matching models is generally not first-best and that search congestion creates wedge-like externalities.

The Hosios condition also became a policy workhorse. It ties optimal labor-market interventions to equilibrium objects (elasticities, bargaining power, vacancy costs), which made matching models attractive for normative macro labor analysis.

## 4. Directed Search and Submarket Equilibrium

Random search treats market tightness as largely aggregate. Directed search, by contrast, allows workers and firms to sort into submarkets with different posted terms. Moen (1997) and Acemoglu and Shimer (1999) show that equilibrium can be organized across posted-wage submarkets, changing both efficiency properties and comparative statics.

This branch sharpens the tension between local equilibrium and aggregate consistency:
- At the submarket level, conditions can look efficient because agents internalize local queues and posting behavior.
- At the aggregate level, composition and spillovers across submarkets can imply nontrivial wedges relative to one-market DMP calibration.

Later quantitative directed-search work extends this insight by embedding heterogeneity and endogenous market segmentation, often finding that representative-tightness summaries can hide important reallocation channels.

## 5. Quantitative Macro Debates and the Dominant Framework

By the 1990s and 2000s, DMP-style search and matching became the dominant frictional labor block in business-cycle and policy models. Merz (1995) and Andolfatto (1996) integrated matching into DSGE-style frameworks. Shimer (2005) then posed the volatility puzzle: standard calibrated DMP with Nash wages implies much smaller unemployment/vacancy fluctuations than observed in U.S. data.

This triggered major responses:
- Hall (2005) emphasized wage rigidity or wage insensitivity as an amplification channel.
- Hagedorn and Manovskii (2008) argued that calibrations with low worker outside options and high value of nonmarket time can generate much larger volatility.
- A broad literature followed on endogenous separations, on-the-job search, alternating-offer wage bargaining, and nominal/real rigidities.

Despite disagreements, the shared baseline remained matching-based equilibrium unemployment with endogenous vacancy creation.

## 6. Representative Position of the Field

A representative summary of the field is:
- There is broad agreement that search and matching frictions are central for interpreting labor market flows and unemployment dynamics.
- There is broad agreement that DMP provides a tractable and conceptually coherent benchmark linking micro frictions to macro outcomes.
- There is ongoing disagreement on which supplementary mechanisms are required to match business-cycle moments and policy elasticities.
- There is sustained methodological tension between one-market aggregate tightness representations and richer submarket equilibrium models with heterogeneous agents.

## 7. Monetary Search and Labor Matching: Points of Contact

The Kiyotaki-Wright tradition and the DMP labor-matching tradition share a common methodological foundation in search theory, yet the two literatures developed largely in parallel before explicit synthesis attempts from the early 2000s onward.

Kiyotaki and Wright (1989, 1993) established that money can emerge endogenously as a medium of exchange in decentralized search economies where agents meet bilaterally, cannot commit to future transfers, and face a double-coincidence-of-wants problem. The matching mechanism is structural: traders search for counterparties, and assets with superior acceptability properties circulate as money in equilibrium. The structural similarity to labor-matching models is direct — both literatures model search as the core friction, both employ Bellman equations over match values, and both face the question of how local bilateral outcomes aggregate to economy-wide equilibria.

Lagos and Wright (2005) provided the tractable platform on which synthesis became practical. Their day/night structure — decentralized bilateral matching in the decentralized market, followed by frictionless centralized trade that resets agent portfolios — produces non-degenerate but analytically manageable money distributions. This architecture made it much easier to embed monetary search alongside richer production and labor-market structure without the endogenous distribution complications that had constrained earlier Kiyotaki-Wright extensions.

Rocheteau and Wright (2005) made the equilibrium-concept connection between the two programs explicit. They showed that the same monetary search environment yields systematically different efficiency and allocative properties depending on whether one applies random search, competitive equilibrium, or competitive search equilibrium. The competitive search version is precisely the submarket concept of Moen (1997) and Acemoglu and Shimer (1999), directly linking the equilibrium frameworks of the monetary and labor-matching literatures.

The most direct synthesis of monetary search and labor matching is Berentsen, Menzio, and Wright (2011). Embedding a Lagos-Wright monetary sector into a Mortensen-Pissarides frictional labor market, the paper derives a full general equilibrium model in which inflation and unemployment are jointly determined in the long run. The transmission mechanism runs through real money balances: higher inflation erodes the purchasing power of cash, tightening workers' participation conditions in goods markets and thereby affecting vacancy-creation profitability and equilibrium unemployment. The long-run Phillips curve is non-vertical in the model for this reason. Menzio's involvement is notable — as a leading figure in directed-search labor models, his contribution brings submarket equilibrium thinking directly into the monetary-labor synthesis.

Shi (1998) provides a related but independently developed monetary propagation framework with production and search-determined employment margins, extending the large-household model (Shi 1995) toward richer real-side interactions.

Williamson and Wright (2010) survey the New Monetarist program and discuss the open research agenda for connecting monetary search to labor and goods markets. Their treatment contextualizes the synthesis efforts above within a broader assessment of frictional general equilibrium economics.

These connections are directly relevant to the submarket-versus-aggregate GE tension at the center of this project. Monetary frictions constitute an additional channel through which local bilateral bargaining and pricing fail to aggregate cleanly into full general equilibrium, and the synthesis literature has exploited this to generate joint predictions about monetary policy and labor-market outcomes that neither tradition could produce alone.
