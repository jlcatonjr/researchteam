## Introduction

In much of business-cycle and policy macroeconomics, labor matching models are treated as a benchmark frictional framework for unemployment dynamics (Pissarides 2000; Shimer 2005). That influence rests on a strong combination of theoretical coherence and quantitative tractability: the framework provides explicit equilibrium conditions for worker search, firm vacancy posting, and wage determination while remaining computationally manageable in aggregate models.

The central tension, however, is persistent. At one level, matching models are built from individual optimization and local equilibrium conditions. At another, macroeconomists want full general equilibrium consistency across production, consumption, asset markets, and policy. The history of the field can be read as repeated attempts to reconcile these two levels without losing either realism or tractability.

## 1. Why Matching Won in Macroeconomics

Labor matching models became dominant because they solved three macro problems at once.

1. Unemployment could be modeled as an equilibrium stock generated by flows.
The DMP class turned unemployment into a law-of-motion object with explicit inflows (job destruction/separation) and outflows (matching/job finding), helping replace ad hoc reduced-form unemployment equations in many macro applications (Mortensen and Pissarides 1994; Pissarides 2000).

2. Vacancy creation became endogenous and policy-relevant.
Firms post vacancies based on expected surplus and entry costs, creating a direct link between shocks, policy wedges, and labor demand in frictional environments.

3. The model has a compact sufficient-statistic representation.
Under random search and common matching-function assumptions, market tightness often summarizes search congestion. That compression made the framework portable across RBC and related DSGE policy-evaluation settings (Merz 1995; Andolfatto 1996).

These features explain why later macro models frequently imported a DMP-style labor block even when the rest of the model architecture differed.

## 2. The Core Equilibrium Logic and Its Limits

In canonical one-market DMP, equilibrium is pinned down by:
- Bellman equations for workers and firms.
- A matching technology and implied hazard rates.
- Free-entry/job-creation condition for vacancies.
- Wage-setting condition, typically Nash bargaining.

This system is internally coherent and, under standard regularity conditions, admits a recursive equilibrium with clear comparative statics. But the same simplifications that deliver tractability also generate limits.

The key limit is aggregation:
- One aggregate market tightness abstracts from submarket composition.
- One wage-bargaining protocol abstracts from contractual institutions.
- Homogeneous pools abstract from worker and job heterogeneity.

These abstractions are often acceptable for baseline policy analysis but can misstate transmission mechanisms when heterogeneity is first-order.

## 3. Submarket Equilibrium Versus Aggregate General Equilibrium

The individualistic versus macro-equilibrium tension is sharpest in directed-search/submarket approaches.

In directed search, firms post contracts (or wages) and workers choose where to queue. Equilibrium conditions are local: each submarket clears through endogenous queue lengths and expected utilities. This yields appealing micro discipline and often stronger efficiency properties than undirected random search under specific assumptions (Moen 1997; Acemoglu and Shimer 1999).

However, macro closure raises complications:
- Submarket entry conditions depend on economy-wide outside options, productivity distributions, and policy wedges.
- Composition effects can move aggregate unemployment/vacancies even when each submarket appears well behaved.
- Mapping rich submarket objects to a single matching function can lose information needed for comparative statics or welfare analysis.

So a model can be internally coherent at the submarket level yet still provide an incomplete macro GE account if product markets, capital accumulation, and household risk-sharing margins are weakly integrated.

## 4. The Volatility Debate as a Stress Test

The Shimer volatility puzzle exposed this interface problem. Standard calibrated DMP with Nash wages generated too little cyclical unemployment and vacancy volatility relative to U.S. data (Shimer 2005). In one reading, this is a calibration detail. In another, it is a sign that local wage and matching conditions are not sufficient to reproduce aggregate dynamics.

Responses took two broad forms.

1. Within-framework modifications:
- Wage rigidity or reduced wage cyclicality (Hall 2005).
- Alternative surplus calibrations and value-of-nonmarket-time assumptions (Hagedorn and Manovskii 2008).
- Endogenous separations, on-the-job search, or bargaining variants.

2. Richer equilibrium environments:
- Stronger integration with capital, financial frictions, or nominal rigidities.
- Heterogeneous-agent structures where distributional dynamics affect outside options and vacancy profitability.
- Full monetary integration: Berentsen, Menzio, and Wright (2011) embed a Lagos-Wright monetary sector (Lagos and Wright 2005) into a Mortensen-Pissarides labor market and show that inflation affects equilibrium unemployment through the real purchasing power of money balances. This is a direct example of a richer GE closure yielding joint dynamics unavailable in either the monetary search or the labor matching framework alone.

The broader lesson in this literature is that matching frictions are often necessary but not sufficient for aggregate labor-market volatility and welfare analysis.

## 5. Efficiency: Local Conditions and Global Welfare

Hosios (1990) remains the organizing benchmark: decentralized search equilibrium can be constrained-efficient when bargaining shares align with matching elasticities. But this is a local condition tied to a specific environment.

In macro applications, global welfare depends on additional margins:
- Tax and transfer systems.
- Monetary/fiscal policy interactions.
- Capital adjustment and intertemporal risk-sharing.
- Heterogeneous worker-firm match quality dynamics.

Hence tension appears again: local search efficiency does not automatically imply macro-policy optimality in full GE. This is one reason the field increasingly treats Hosios as a benchmark condition rather than a complete policy rule.

## 6. Representative Synthesis of Field Development

A representative and accurate summary of developments is:

1. Early search theory established equilibrium unemployment with frictions.
2. DMP provided the dominant unified framework for unemployment, vacancies, job flows, and wage setting.
3. Directed search and submarket models clarified that equilibrium structure can be richer than one-market tightness summaries.
4. Quantitative macro debates (especially volatility and wage cyclicality) revealed that matching blocks require complementary mechanisms for empirical fit.
5. The current dominant approach is plural within a shared core: most models keep matching-based job creation/destruction, then differ on wage institutions, heterogeneity, and broader GE integration.

The field has not broadly rejected matching. Instead, it has moved from a single benchmark to a family of matching-consistent macro frameworks, each making different tradeoffs between micro detail and aggregate tractability.
