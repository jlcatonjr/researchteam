# Research Plan: Coded Term Definitions and Synced Term-Occurrence Indexing

Prepared by: @topic-scoping-expert  
Date: November 16, 2026  
Output directory: Projects/CodedTermIndexing/

## Research Question

What role does a machine-readable ("coded") register of term definitions, joined to an automatically
maintained ("synced") index of every occurrence of each registered term across a body of documents, play in
enforcing consistent usage? The question has two parts:

1. How does the academic literature treat such registers and indexes?
2. Which existing infrastructures and field guides take this approach, and how do they keep the index in
   step with the documents?

The findings feed a design decision for this framework: how researchteam projects should define their terms,
index their occurrences and keep the index current.

## Working Definitions

These four terms carry the argument. Their canonical definitions are the coded ones in
`terminology/terms.json`, which this list restates. They are used in one sense throughout the project.

- **Coded definition.** A term definition held as structured data (an identifier, a preferred label, a
  definition and its listed variants), so that a program can read it.
- **Term register.** The set of coded definitions a project maintains.
- **Occurrence index.** A derived record of every place a given form appears in a body of documents. In
  this project's design, the forms are a registered term and its listed variants, and the documents are
  the project's own.
- **Synced.** Regenerated by automatic triggers as the documents or the register change, with any
  remaining staleness detectable by a check.

## Scope

In scope:

- Terminology science and standards: concept-oriented term records, preferred and non-preferred terms.
- Knowledge organization: controlled vocabularies and vocabulary control.
- The history of occurrence-level indexing: concordances, keyword-in-context indexes, back-of-book indexes.
- Concept formation in the social sciences and codebooks in qualitative research, as the scholarly
  practices closest to a research project's own term register.
- Software and requirements engineering: glossaries, identifier dictionaries, documentation consistency.
- Derived-artifact freshness: how build systems, hooks and continuous integration keep a generated artifact
  current.
- Existing infrastructures and field guides: documentation toolchains, markup standards, prose linters,
  terminology tools and published style guides.

Out of scope:

- Automatic term extraction as a research field in its own right, beyond what bears on maintaining a
  register.
- Machine translation and multilingual termbase exchange, beyond the record structure they standardise.
- Ontology engineering and formal reasoning over definitions.
- Evaluation of commercial products beyond what their public documentation states.

## Method and Selection Criteria

1. Prefer peer-reviewed articles, monographs from scholarly presses and published standards.
2. Confirm every DOI against the CrossRef record before the source enters the bibliography.
3. Record evidence as verbatim quotations with the address they were retrieved from, in
   `references/evidence/`. A claim in a deliverable must trace to a quotation there.
4. Record the access level reached for each source: full text, abstract only, or publisher description
   only. A claim may not go beyond the level reached.
5. Treat tool documentation and style guides as **primary evidence of what the tool or guide says**, not as
   scholarly findings. The framework's authority hierarchy ranks scholarly repositories first. The operator
   asked for existing infrastructures and field guides, and their own documentation is the only direct
   evidence of their behaviour. Deliverables keep the two kinds of source in separate sections.
6. Mark anything that could not be confirmed as `UNVERIFIED` and keep it out of the argument.

## Key Analytical Axes

1. **Register.** What is recorded for each term, and whether the record is concept-oriented.
2. **Occurrence handling.** Whether occurrences are marked by the author, detected by matching, or both.
3. **Index.** Whether the system produces a list of every occurrence, and who reads it.
4. **Enforcement.** Whether inconsistent usage is reported, and whether the report blocks anything.
5. **Sync trigger.** What causes the index to be regenerated, and how staleness is detected.

Every factual claim is paired with a citation, and every citation with a verified record and a source link.
The audit establishes that the project is well formed. It does not prove that no claim is mistaken, and
residual `UNVERIFIED` items are listed for human resolution.
